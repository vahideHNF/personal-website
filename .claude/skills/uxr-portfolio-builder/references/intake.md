# Intake: getting the material

The quality of the portfolio is capped by the material. Your job here is to get the facts you need with the least effort from the person, and to never fill gaps with fiction.

## Contents
1. What you need (minimum and ideal)
2. Mining what they give you
3. Asking questions
4. Choosing the case studies
5. Confidentiality
6. When facts are missing

## 1. What you need

**Minimum to start building**

| For | Facts |
|---|---|
| Site | Full name, role title, email; LinkedIn URL if they have one |
| Resume | Each role: title, employer, start and end month, city; education; skills and tools |
| Each case study (2-4) | The problem (ideally with a number), what they did (methods, sample sizes), the key finding, what changed because of it |
| Policy | Can employers be named? Are numbers real, changed, or hidden? |

**Ideal additions**: the measured result after launch; a surprising finding; hypotheses the team had before; quotes they may share; what's next; dates and team; years of experience; target roles or job ads; a colour they like; their GitHub username (for the address).

## 2. Mining what they give you

Read everything attached before asking anything. Extract into your plan, not into chat.

- **CV or LinkedIn PDF** → resume entries, dates, education, skills, languages, certificates, mentoring, volunteering. LinkedIn "About" often holds a usable intro voice. Old CV bullets are usually task lists ("Conducted interviews"); rewrite them as outcomes later, and ask for the missing result if the project is important.
- **Research reports, decks, one-pagers** → the case-study material:
  - research questions → the problem and the case title;
  - method and sample ("2,300 survey responses", "15 usability sessions") → captions and the "how we found out" step;
  - findings with numbers → bars, rings, splits;
  - segment comparisons → the `vs` and paired bars;
  - "we assumed X but found Y" → the surprise step;
  - recommendations and their status → the "In one glance" list;
  - post-launch metrics or A/B results → the impact step and the card number.
  Reports are often confidential: check the policy before reusing any figure.
- **Interview notes, transcripts, survey exports** → quotes (anonymise: role and context only, "Driver, 6 years on the app") and theme frequencies ("9 of 14 participants").
- **Job ads they're targeting** → which studies to feature and which keywords to make sure the resume uses truthfully (methods, tools, domains).
- **An existing portfolio** → reuse content, not layout. Note what they liked about it.

## 3. Asking questions

Ask in at most two rounds, each short enough to answer in a few minutes. Number the questions so people can answer "3: yes". Offer examples of good answers; people answer better when they see the shape. If the platform has a multiple-choice question tool, use it for the policy questions; keep open questions in plain chat.

**Round 1: essentials and policy** (only what's missing)
1. Name as it should appear, role title, city and country, email, LinkedIn.
2. Which 3-4 projects are you proudest of, or would most like to talk about in an interview?
3. Numbers: can the site show your real figures, should we change them and say so ("illustrative"), or keep them out? Is the resume allowed to show real measured results?
4. Can we name your employers and products, or should we describe them ("a ride-hailing platform")?
5. Roles you're applying for (or paste a job ad you like).
6. Any colour you like, or a logo? (Optional; there is a good default.)

**Round 2: per case study gaps** (ask about the 2-3 facts that would most change the story)
- "What did the team believe before your research?" (fuels the hypotheses step)
- "What surprised you?" (fuels the turn of the story)
- "What exactly shipped, and what happened to the numbers after?" (the impact step and the card)
- "How many people did you survey, interview or test with?" (credibility in captions)
- "Is there one sentence a participant said that sums it up?" (a quote)

Stop asking once you can tell each story honestly. Three strong studies with gaps written around beat four studies waiting on answers.

## 4. Choosing the case studies

Pick 3-4 (2 is fine early in a career; 6 is the ceiling). Prefer, in order:
1. **Evidence of impact**: research that changed a decision or a product, ideally measured.
2. **A spread of methods**: e.g. one quantitative-led, one qualitative-led, one mixed or evaluative.
3. **Relevance** to the roles they want (domain, B2B vs consumer, seniority signals like leading or influencing strategy).
4. **Recency**.

Order the cards strongest first. Older or smaller projects belong on the resume, not on the home page.

Junior researchers, career changers and students: bootcamp, academic, volunteer and self-initiated projects are fine. Say plainly what they were, and make the impact what changed (a team's decision, a design iteration, a learning) rather than a business metric.

## 5. Confidentiality

Ask; never assume. The policy can differ by employer (an old employer may have published results that a current one keeps under NDA); apply each policy to its own pages and say which studies are illustrative. Then apply it consistently:

| Policy | Case-study pages | Home cards | Resume |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real numbers allowed** | Real figures, no NDA note | Real headline number | Real results |
| **Changed numbers** (common for in-house researchers) | Illustrative figures that keep the direction and rough size of every effect, plus the NDA note on each page | Illustrative number + the note under the cards | Real measured results, **only if** the person confirms the resume may show them; otherwise relative wording |
| **No numbers** | Relative language ("about half", "most", "twice as many") and directions; visuals use counts the person can share (participants, sessions) | Use a scale or outcome number ("37 interviews", "3 segments") | Relative wording |

Rules for illustrative numbers: change the values, keep every direction (up stays up), keep the rough magnitude, never make them more impressive than the real ones, and use the same illustrative value everywhere it appears.

Sample sizes (participants, responses) and public facts already on the CV (user counts, team sizes) are usually not confidential, and changing them weakens credibility. Keep them real by default, but say so in the hand-over and offer to change them.

Also remove or generalise: internal project and feature code names, dashboard and tool names that reveal internals, colleague names (use roles), participant names (use role and context), unreleased features, city or customer names if the person flags them, and anything in screenshots (blur, or use the phone mock-up component instead).

Employer naming can change later (the original site switched from "a ride-hailing platform" to the company name); keep naming in the kicker and card label so it's easy to change.

## 6. When facts are missing

- **Write around minor gaps**: omit the number and keep the finding, use the scale you do know, or drop the step.
- **Never placeholder-in-production**: the checker fails on `{{…}}`, so a gap is either answered, written around, or the section is removed.
- **No measured impact?** Use what did change: a decision taken or reversed, a feature redesigned, a roadmap item added or dropped, a team that now runs the survey every quarter. Mark it in the status list honestly (`tested`, `dev`, `later`).
- **Keep a running list** of assumptions and gaps, and give it to the person at hand-over so they can correct you.
