Ride-hailing · Periodic driver survey · 2025–2026

What do drivers need to know before they say yes to a long trip?

Long-distance rides were growing, but nobody had a reliable picture of the drivers behind them. I ran a driver survey, then repeated it nine months later to see what our fixes had actually changed. Round 1 reshaped the ride card and bidding. Round 2 showed the bottleneck had moved, and set the next round of work.

In short

  • Problem
    There was no measured picture of why drivers took, skipped or cancelled long-distance rides, and no way to tell whether things were getting better.
  • Insight
    Half of drivers couldn't decide from the ride card. Once that was fixed, competition between drivers and passenger cancellations took its place: every fix moved the bottleneck.
  • Impact
    Passenger and luggage details on the ride card: 11× luggage-preference use, +1.9% acceptance, −2.0% driver cancellations. A verified-passenger badge added +1.6% acceptance.

01 — The problemLots of opinions, no baseline

Long-distance rides work differently from city rides. A driver commits hours to one trip, often far from home, with a stranger and their luggage. Whether drivers say yes depends on money, distance, safety and whether they can find a ride back.

Earlier studies had each looked at one slice of the problem, and nothing showed whether things were getting better or worse over time. The team needed a repeatable measure of the driver experience, not another one-off study.

02 — My roleWhat I owned

I ran both rounds. I wrote the research questions with the product managers, designed the questionnaire, defined the sample, analysed the results and broke them down by driver persona. In Round 2 I kept the core questions and the sample definition identical, added questions for the new features, and reported what had changed rather than a fresh snapshot.

03 — ApproachSame four questions, asked twice

  • Adoption: how often, and through which entry point, do drivers take long-distance rides?
  • Barriers: what stops drivers from accepting, or makes them cancel?
  • Features: is the ride information enough to decide? How do drivers feel about bidding?
  • Safety: how safe do drivers feel on long-distance trips?

Each round went to a random sample of drivers approved for long-distance rides who had finished at least one in the past 45 days, and got about 2,000 valid responses. Breaking results down by persona (full-time drivers, older drivers and part-timers who drive to top up their income) mattered, because the groups often behaved in opposite ways.

Limitation: a repeated survey shows what changed, not what caused it. Several features launched between rounds, so each shift was treated as a lead to test, and every shipped change was measured on real rides.

04 — Round 1What drivers told us first

Money and empty returns keep drivers away

About one in six whitelisted drivers took no long-distance ride in the previous month. Low fares were the top reason, but nearly as many feared driving home empty. That second reason doesn't go away with a higher fare.

Half of drivers couldn't decide from the ride card

47% said the offer didn't tell them enough to accept with confidence. Their wish list was concrete: how many passengers and how much luggage, a more specific origin and destination (the village or district, not just the city), total trip time, a realistic fare including tolls, and signs the passenger was verified.

This explained a pattern from an earlier study: drivers accepted a ride, called the passenger, found four or five people with luggage instead of three, and cancelled.

Bidding: almost everyone tried it, half were happy

82% of drivers had tried bidding, but only 51% were satisfied. They bid blind, with no idea whether a price would win. The personas reacted in opposite ways to a rejected bid: part-timers often gave up on the ride entirely, while older drivers lowered their price and tried again. One fix wouldn't serve both groups.

A safety baseline: 3.8 out of 5

Drivers rated their safety 3.8 out of 5, the number later rounds would be compared against. The experiences that made them feel unsafe fell into three groups: fear of crime, payments that didn't arrive, and passengers whose identity they couldn't verify (often someone booking for another person). Full-time drivers felt the safest.

06 — Round 2, nine months laterThe bottleneck moved

Between the rounds, drivers got scheduled rides, destination alerts and a bid win-chance indicator. Launch dashboards showed people used them, but not whether drivers' experience as a whole had improved.

Long-distance rides became a habit, and money stopped being the main complaint

More drivers said most of their rides were now long-distance (24% → 31%). Fare was still the biggest barrier, but it was cited less often, and cancellations because the fare didn't match the distance dropped sharply.

But new problems took their place

As fare complaints fell, two other problems grew. Passengers cancelling or changing plans became a more common reason for drivers to cancel, which we linked to the new scheduled rides (a passenger who books days ahead has more time to change their mind). And competition between drivers became the main complaint about bidding and the long-distance ride list. More drivers felt someone else always accepted the ride first.

Bid win-chance kept drivers trying

After a rejected bid, fewer drivers abandoned the ride (29% → 21%). More of them retried or moved on to another ride. Satisfaction with bidding overall held steady, but the complaints changed from "the bidding feature is confusing" to "too many drivers are bidding", which is a marketplace problem, not a design one.

Safety fears changed shape

Overall, drivers' sense of safety held steady, and slightly more of them felt completely safe. But what worried them changed. Fear of crime almost halved, while distrust about payments and unverified passengers grew. Drivers were less afraid of being hurt and more afraid of not being paid or not knowing who they were driving.

One problem that didn't move: driving home empty. It was the only major barrier that didn't improve at all, so it became the top priority.

08 — What I learnedReflection

A baseline is only worth something if you repeat it.Keeping the core questions and the sample definition the same is what made Round 2 a real comparison rather than a new study.
Every fix moves the bottleneck.The most useful thing Round 2 found was how solving fares exposed competition and passenger reliability. Without a repeated survey, those would have looked like unrelated complaints.
Averages hide opposite behaviours.Without persona breakdowns, the bidding finding would have read as "drivers are lukewarm". In fact one group quits and another keeps trying, and they need different fixes.
