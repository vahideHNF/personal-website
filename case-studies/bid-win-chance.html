Bidding blind: making long-distance bidding fair for drivers and passengers

On long-distance trips, drivers can bid a price instead of accepting the standard fare. Drivers were bidding without knowing what would win, and passengers were getting offers at two to three times the normal fare. Research on both sides of the marketplace led to a bid win-chance indicator and driver tiers, and bid prices came down.

In short

  • Problem
    Most drivers had tried bidding but only half liked it, and passengers were complaining about inflated prices. Both sides were losing trust in the same feature.
  • Insight
    Drivers weren't greedy, they were blind: with no feedback on what wins, they guessed high. And a single bid limit for every car felt unfair to drivers of better cars.
  • Impact
    A live win-chance indicator and driver tiers shipped: −5.5% average bid price, +3.6% bids per request, −0.7% passenger cancellations.

01 — The problemA marketplace where both sides were unhappy

For long-distance trips, a passenger requests a ride and drivers can either accept the standard fare or bid their own price. The passenger then picks an offer. Bidding gives drivers a fair price on long routes, while passengers get choice. When it works, everyone wins.

It wasn't working. In the first long-distance driver survey, 82% of drivers had tried bidding, but only 51% were satisfied. On the passenger side, about four in five passengers who were unhappy with long-distance rides said drivers bid two to three times the standard fare. Rejected bids also wasted time for both sides: drivers waited for an answer that never came, and passengers scrolled past offers they would never accept.

The question was what was going wrong in the bidding moment, for each side, and what would fix it without just capping prices.

02 — My roleWhat I owned

I led the research on both sides of the marketplace across several studies: the bidding questions in the periodic driver survey, a passenger survey on long-distance pricing, and the research team's first conjoint study on what passengers weigh when choosing a bid. I turned the findings into recommendations for the product team, and used the next survey round to check what had changed after launch.

03 — ApproachLook at the same moment from both sides

  • Drivers (survey, about 2,000 responses): how they bid, why they were unhappy, and what they did after a rejected bid, broken down by driver persona.
  • Passengers (survey): how they judged driver offers and what made them reject bids or cancel.
  • Passengers (conjoint): passengers chose between realistic bid cards that varied price, pickup time, car type and the driver's number of completed rides. This showed how much each factor really drives the choice, instead of asking people what they think matters.

Limitation: surveys and conjoint show what people say and choose in a controlled task. So every shipped change was checked with A/B tests on real rides.

04 — What we foundFour findings

1. Drivers were bidding blind

Drivers had no idea whether a price would win, so many overbid and never heard back. Different driver groups reacted to a rejected bid in opposite ways: part-timers often gave up on the ride, while older drivers lowered their price and tried again. None of them got any feedback to learn from.

2. One bid limit for every car felt unfair

Every driver had the same bidding limit, whatever their car. Drivers saw this as unfair and inflexible: a newer, more comfortable car and an old one were capped the same way. One rule for everyone was too strict for some drivers and too loose for others.

3. Passengers choose on price and pickup time, not driver history

The conjoint study showed price dominated passengers' choice, followed by how soon the driver could arrive and the car type. The driver's number of completed rides, which the team planned to add to the bid card, had almost no effect. That feature was dropped before anyone built it, which saved engineering and product time.

4. Overpricing was a feedback problem, not a greed problem

Put together, the two sides explained each other. Drivers guessed high because they had no signal, passengers saw the high guesses and lost trust, and drivers then got rejected and gave up. The fix wasn't a lower cap. It was giving drivers the information passengers were already using to choose.

The narrower price range had a clear trade-off: fewer requests received a bid and acceptance dipped slightly, but the rides that were accepted were more likely to happen (+3.2% finish/accept, +1.5% fulfillment, both illustrative). Both sides of that trade-off were reported, so the team could decide on it knowingly.

The next driver survey showed the change from the drivers' side: fewer drivers abandoned a ride after a rejected bid (29% → 21%), and complaints about the bid limit fell. The main complaint became competition from other drivers, the next problem to solve (see the driver survey).

07 — What I learnedReflection

In a marketplace, research both sides of the same moment.The driver survey alone said "drivers want higher limits". The passenger data alone said "drivers overcharge". Together they showed a missing feedback loop, which neither side could see.
Measure what people do, not what they say matters.Asked directly, passengers might well say a driver's experience matters. The conjoint showed it barely moved their choices, and that saved a feature from being built.
Name the trade-offs.The price-range test improved finished rides but lowered bid coverage. Showing both sides of the result kept the decision with the team, where it belongs.
