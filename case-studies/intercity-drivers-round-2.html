Nine months later: what changed for intercity drivers, and what our fixes broke

After the first survey, the intercity team shipped scheduled rides, destination alerts and a bid win-chance indicator. I ran the same survey again to see what had actually changed. Some problems shrank, new ones took their place, and the results set the next round of work.

In short

  • Question
    Did the features shipped after Round 1 improve the intercity driver experience, and what new problems appeared?
  • Insight
    Fare complaints fell, but competition between drivers and passenger cancellations rose. The fixes had moved the bottleneck, not removed it.
  • Impact
    A verified-passenger badge shipped (+1.6% ride acceptance), and in-ride return booking and multiple bidding followed.

01 — The problemShipping isn't the same as solving

Between the two rounds, drivers got three big changes: scheduled rides, destination alerts for new requests, and a bid win-chance indicator. Each one targeted something Round 1 had found. But launch dashboards only show whether people use a feature, not whether drivers' experience as a whole improved, or whether one fix created a new problem somewhere else.

02 — My roleWhat I owned

I ran Round 2 with the same core questions and the same sample definition as Round 1, so the two rounds could be compared directly. I also added questions for the new features. I analysed what had changed, not just the current numbers, and wrote recommendations ranked by impact and effort.

03 — ApproachSame questions, same drivers, new answers

A random sample of whitelisted drivers who had finished an intercity ride in the past 45 days, the same definition as Round 1, gave about 2,000 valid responses (95% confidence, around ±2 points). For every question I compared Round 2 with Round 1 and reported only the shifts that were bigger than the margin of error.

Limitation: a repeated survey shows what changed, not what caused it. Several features launched in the same period, so I treated each shift as a strong lead to test, not as proof.

04 — What we foundThe bottleneck moved

1. Intercity became a habit, and money stopped being the main complaint

More drivers said most of their rides were now intercity (24% → 31%). Fare was still the biggest barrier, but it was cited less often, and cancellations because the fare didn't match the distance dropped sharply.

2. But new problems took their place

As fare complaints fell, two other problems grew. Passengers cancelling or changing plans became a more common reason for drivers to cancel, which we linked to the new scheduled rides (a passenger who books days ahead has more time to change their mind). And competition between drivers became the main complaint about bidding and the intercity list. More drivers felt someone else always accepted the ride first.

3. Bid win-chance kept drivers trying

After a rejected bid, fewer drivers abandoned the ride (29% → 21%). More of them retried or moved on to another ride. Satisfaction with bidding overall held steady, but the complaints changed from "the bidding feature is confusing" to "too many drivers are bidding", which is a marketplace problem, not a design one.

4. Safety fears changed shape

Overall, drivers' sense of safety held steady, and slightly more of them felt completely safe. But what worried them changed. Fear of crime almost halved, while distrust about payments and unverified passengers grew. Drivers were less afraid of being hurt and more afraid of not being paid or not knowing who they were driving.

One problem that didn't move: driving home empty. It was the only major barrier that didn't improve at all, so it became the top priority.

07 — What I learnedReflection

Every fix moves the bottleneck.The most useful thing Round 2 found wasn't a new problem. It was seeing how solving fares exposed competition and passenger reliability. Without a repeated survey, those would have looked like unrelated complaints.
Report the change, not the snapshot.A standalone percentage invites "is that good or bad?". Organising the whole report around shifts since Round 1 answered that question before anyone asked it.
Be honest about cause.Several features launched at once, so each shift was framed as a lead to test, with the fix and how to measure it named alongside. That kept the recommendations credible.
