Same drivers, different week: why drivers cancel scheduled rides they just accepted

Scheduled intercity rides let passengers book a trip days ahead. Too many drivers were cancelling them within two minutes of accepting. I found out why, and the fix that shipped first cut passenger cancellations and raised finished rides.

My role
UX Researcher (lead)
Team
Intercity PMs, designers, data analyst
Timeline

Methods

In short

  • Problem
    About half of after-accept cancellations on scheduled rides happened within minutes, and we didn't know which of many known pain points was causing them.
  • Insight
    Quick cancellers weren't a different kind of driver. What set them apart was having been burned by a passenger cancelling on them before.
  • Impact
    Passenger contact details after acceptance shipped first: −3.1% passenger cancellations before the start, +15% finished rides.

01 — The problemA promise drivers stopped trusting

Scheduled rides were meant to make intercity trips predictable for both sides: the passenger books Tehran → Isfahan for Friday, a driver accepts on Tuesday, and both can plan. In practice, a large share of drivers cancelled minutes after accepting.

Two earlier qualitative studies had already produced a long list of suspects. The ride card showed no passenger information after acceptance. Nobody had told drivers that the passenger's number appears only 15 minutes before departure. A fare range made it hard to plan income on long routes. Passengers cancelled at the last minute. Drivers couldn't book a return trip while on a ride, so many drove home empty. And nothing stopped a driver from dropping a scheduled ride when a better on-demand ride came along.

Every item on that list was plausible, and the team couldn't build all of them. The real question was which problems actually drive cancellations, and how common each one is.

02 — My roleWhat I owned

I led the study end to end: framing the research questions with the Intercity PMs, defining the two driver segments from ride data, designing the survey, analysing it, and turning the findings into recommendations ranked by impact and effort.

03 — ApproachCompare the drivers who cancel with the ones who finish

Asking only cancellers "why did you cancel?" gives you a list of reasons but no way to tell which ones matter. So I compared two groups of intercity drivers from the same week:

  • Finishers: drivers who finished their scheduled rides.
  • Quick cancellers: drivers who cancelled a scheduled ride within two minutes of accepting it.

If a pain point is common in both groups, it's an annoyance. If it's much more common among quick cancellers, it's a lead. The survey went to a random sample from each segment and got about 2,300 valid responses, which was enough to compare the groups with a margin of error under 4 points.

Limitation: a survey shows association, not cause. That's why the first recommendation was something we could ship quickly and measure with an experiment.

04 — What we foundThree findings that reshaped the roadmap

1. Quick cancellers aren't a different type of driver

I expected the two groups to differ in age, experience or how much they rely on intercity work. They didn't. Their stated reasons for disliking scheduled rides were almost the same too: uncompetitive fares and not being able to bid came up equally in both groups.

2. What separates them is having been burned by a passenger

The one big gap was exposure to passenger cancellations. Quick cancellers were 14 points more likely to name a passenger cancelling on them as a bad experience, and they were far more likely to rate scheduled rides "very bad" (31% vs 20%). Drivers who'd been let down before were treating every new booking as likely to fall through, so they dropped it the moment something more certain appeared.

3. Drivers wanted certainty more than money

The fare compared with on-demand rides was the least valued part of scheduled rides. What drivers valued was being able to plan, and quick cancellers cared even more about planning their daily income. A feature built to give drivers certainty was failing exactly there.

Two things we could stop worrying about. Passengers changing the start time was barely a problem (under 8% in either group), so schedule flexibility dropped down the roadmap. And the feature hardly helped with return trips: only about 3% of drivers' return rides came through it, while around four times as many drivers found return passengers outside Snapp. That return demand already exists, and we weren't capturing it.

07 — What I learnedReflection

Compare groups, don't just list reasons.Surveying only cancellers would have given us the same long list we already had. Comparing them with finishers turned the list into a ranking.
Look for the dull result too."These drivers aren't different" sounded like a non-finding, but it ruled out a whole set of driver-targeted fixes and pointed us at the passenger side.
Ship something measurable first.Leading with a lower-effort change that could be measured quickly gave the team evidence early, while the bigger pricing changes were still being built.
